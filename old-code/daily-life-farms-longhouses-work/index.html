Lesson 05 / Daily life / Work

Farms, longhouses
and work

The voyages became famous. Everyday labour made them possible.

R3X Studio7 min readUpdated July 2026

In this lesson

Most people in Viking-age Scandinavia did not spend their lives at sea. Their world was built around farms, households, seasonal work and the constant need to produce food, clothing, tools and shelter.

Life on the farm

The farm was the basic centre of production. Depending on region and wealth, households cultivated barley, rye and oats, kept cattle, sheep, goats and pigs, and used nearby woodland, coastlines and rivers. Fishing, hunting and gathering supplemented agriculture.

A farm could include barns, stables, workshops and storage buildings as well as the main dwelling. Larger estates controlled more land and labour; modest farms operated on a smaller scale. Landscape and climate shaped what each household could produce.

Inside the longhouse

The longhouse was usually built of wood, earth and other local materials. Internal posts supported the roof, while a central hearth supplied heat, light and a place to cook. Smoke escaped imperfectly, and the interior could be dark, crowded and smoky.

The building was not simply a private family home in the modern sense. It was a shared working space where people cooked, repaired equipment, processed food, made textiles, received guests and sometimes sheltered animals.

The longhouse was home, workshop, storehouse and social stage.

A world of work

Daily life demanded skill. People spun wool, wove cloth, worked wood and iron, repaired roofs, preserved food and maintained boats. Textile production alone required cleaning fibre, spinning thread, weaving and sewing—hours of labour behind every sail and garment.

Work was divided by status, age, gender and local custom, but simple modern labels can mislead. Women often managed household production and textile work; men commonly performed heavy agricultural, construction and maritime tasks. Enslaved people carried out domestic, agricultural and other demanding labour without freedom.

Season and survival

The year organised life. Spring preparation, summer grazing and growing, autumn harvest and winter repair created a rhythm that expeditions had to fit around. A failed harvest or loss of animals could threaten a household, while surplus supported trade, feasts and political power.

Understanding this everyday economy changes the picture of the Viking Age. Ships, weapons and silver rested on food production, craft knowledge and communities that kept working while some members travelled.
